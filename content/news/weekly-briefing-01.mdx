## Introduction

Rice is a dietary staple for more than half the world's population, but it carries a hidden burden: arsenic. Unlike most crops, rice is grown in flooded paddies where soil conditions mobilize arsenic into a form the plant readily absorbs. The result is that rice and rice-based products consistently contain higher concentrations of arsenic—particularly the more toxic inorganic form—than virtually any other grain. So how dangerous is arsenic in rice, really? The answer depends on how much you eat, what type of rice you choose, and how you prepare it.

## Why Rice Accumulates More Arsenic Than Other Foods

Arsenic is a naturally occurring element found in soil, water, and air. It exists in two primary forms: organic arsenic (less toxic) and inorganic arsenic (more toxic and classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the IARC). Rice paddies create anaerobic, waterlogged conditions that convert soil-bound arsenic into soluble arsenite, which rice roots absorb through their silicon transport pathways. This mechanism makes rice uniquely efficient at concentrating arsenic compared to wheat, corn, and other cereals grown in dry soil. Climate change may worsen the problem: rising temperatures and CO₂ levels alter the soil microbiome in ways that increase arsenic accumulation in rice.

## How Much Arsenic Is Actually in Rice?

According to FDA testing data on over 650 rice products, average inorganic arsenic concentrations are approximately 92 parts per billion (ppb) in white rice and 154 ppb in brown rice. Brown rice contains roughly 50–80% more inorganic arsenic than white rice of the same variety because the bran and germ layers—where arsenic concentrates—are removed during the milling of white rice. A 2025 investigation by Healthy Babies, Bright Futures found that one in four rice samples from over 100 U.S. store brands exceeded the FDA's 100 ppb action level for infant rice cereal. U.S.-grown brown rice samples averaged 151 ppb total heavy metals, with 129 ppb from arsenic alone.

## What Are the Health Risks?

The health risks of chronic arsenic exposure are well established. Inorganic arsenic is strongly linked to cancers of the lung, bladder, and skin, as well as ischemic heart disease. Emerging evidence also connects arsenic exposure to type 2 diabetes, adverse pregnancy outcomes (including miscarriage, stillbirth, and preterm birth), neurodevelopmental issues in children, and immune system dysfunction. Dietary arsenic from rice can reach concentrations equivalent to or exceeding the U.S. EPA's maximum contaminant level for drinking water (10 μg/L). The risks are highest for populations that consume rice daily as a primary calorie source, as well as infants, young children, pregnant women, and people on gluten-free diets that rely heavily on rice-based substitutes.

## Current Regulatory Standards

In 2020, the FDA finalized an action level of 100 ppb inorganic arsenic specifically for infant rice cereals, which led to a 45% reduction in arsenic levels found in those products. However, the FDA has not yet established action levels for arsenic in rice sold for general cooking and consumption—a significant regulatory gap. The European Commission has set tiered limits: 100 ppb for rice intended for infants and young children, 200 ppb for non-parboiled white rice, 250 ppb for parboiled and husked rice, and 300 ppb for rice-based snacks like crackers and cakes. The Codex Alimentarius Commission (WHO/FAO) has set a maximum level of 200 ppb for polished rice. Advocacy groups like Healthy Babies, Bright Futures are calling on the FDA to extend arsenic limits to all rice products—not just infant cereals.

## How to Reduce Your Arsenic Exposure from Rice

You don't have to eliminate rice from your diet, but there are practical steps to significantly lower your arsenic intake:

1. **Choose lower-arsenic varieties.** Basmati rice from India, Pakistan, and California, as well as sushi rice from the U.S., tend to have lower arsenic levels. Rice grown in the south-central U.S. (Arkansas, Louisiana, Texas) tends to be higher.
2. **Rinse and cook with excess water.** Cooking rice in a 6:1 or higher water-to-rice ratio and draining the excess can remove up to 30–60% of inorganic arsenic.
3. **Opt for white over brown when arsenic is a concern.** While brown rice has more fiber and nutrients, it also has significantly more arsenic.
4. **Diversify your grains.** Rotate rice with quinoa, millet, buckwheat, oats, and other grains that accumulate far less arsenic.
5. **Limit rice-based products for infants.** Follow FDA guidance on infant rice cereals and consider oat-based or multi-grain alternatives.
6. **Look for third-party heavy metal testing.** Products certified through independent testing programs provide additional assurance that arsenic levels have been evaluated.

## The Bottom Line

Arsenic in rice is a legitimate food safety concern, not a scare tactic. The science is clear that chronic exposure to inorganic arsenic—even at low levels—carries meaningful health risks. While rice remains a nutritious food that can be part of a healthy diet, consumers should be informed about which types of rice carry higher risk and how preparation methods can reduce exposure. The regulatory landscape is evolving, but significant gaps remain—particularly the absence of FDA action levels for rice sold to the general population. Until those gaps are closed, third-party heavy metal testing and certification programs play an essential role in protecting consumers.
